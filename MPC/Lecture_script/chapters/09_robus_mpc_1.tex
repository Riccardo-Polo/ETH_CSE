\section{Motivation}

The previous chapters developed MPC under the assumption that the prediction model used by the
controller describes the plant exactly. At each sampling instant, the controller predicts the
future evolution of the system, optimizes a finite-horizon cost, and applies the first input of the
optimal sequence. This nominal viewpoint is useful and forms the basis of the theory developed
so far, but it hides an important practical difficulty: real systems are never described perfectly
by the model used inside the optimizer.\\

Model errors may have several origins. Some parameters may be unknown or only approximately
identified, the mathematical model may neglect nonlinear effects, and the plant may be affected
by disturbances or noise. Therefore, even if the nominal prediction satisfies all constraints, the
real trajectory may deviate from it. This deviation can lead to constraint violations, degraded
performance, or even loss of feasibility of the MPC problem at later time instants.\\
Robust MPC addresses this issue by explicitly incorporating uncertainty into the control
design. The goal is no longer only to optimize the nominal predicted trajectory, but to guarantee
that the real system behaves acceptably for all admissible uncertainty realizations. In particular,
we would like the closed-loop system to satisfy constraints for all possible disturbances, to remain
stable in a suitable sense, and to retain good performance despite model mismatch.\\
A key difference with nominal MPC is that persistent disturbances generally prevent convergence
to a single equilibrium point. If the disturbance does not vanish asymptotically, then the state
cannot be expected to converge exactly to the origin. The best one can typically hope for is
convergence to a neighborhood of the origin, whose size depends on the magnitude of the
uncertainty and on the robustness properties of the controller.\\

The central question of this chapter is therefore the following. If the controller predicts the
future using a nominal model, but the real system is affected by bounded disturbances, how can
we modify the MPC formulation so that the real state still respects the constraints?

\section{Uncertainty Models}

\subsection{MPC predictions and the real system}

In nominal predictive control, the system is assumed to evolve according to a deterministic model
of the form
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),u(k)).
\]
This means that, once the current state and the future input sequence are fixed, the future state
trajectory is uniquely determined. The optimizer can therefore compute a single predicted
trajectory and impose constraints directly on that trajectory.\\
In the real world, however, the evolution of the system is better described by a model of the
form
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),u(k),w(k);\theta),
\]
where \(w(k)\) represents a time-varying disturbance or noise term, while \(\theta\) denotes
unknown model parameters. The disturbance \(w(k)\) changes from one sampling instant to the
next, whereas \(\theta\) is often assumed to be unknown but constant, or slowly varying. This
means that the future state is no longer uniquely determined by the current state and the planned
input sequence. Instead, each possible realization of the uncertainty gives rise to a different
trajectory.\\

This distinction is essential. In nominal MPC, one checks whether the predicted trajectory belongs
to the constraint sets. In robust MPC, one must check whether all possible trajectories generated
by the uncertainty belong to the constraint sets. Thus, the objects to be propagated are no longer
single trajectories, but sets of possible trajectories.

\subsection{Goals of robust constrained control}

Consider the uncertain constrained system
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),u(k),w(k);\theta),
\]
with constraints
\[
    x(k) \in \mathcal{X}, \qquad u(k) \in \mathcal{U},
\]
and uncertainty sets
\[
    w(k) \in \mathcal{W}, \qquad \theta \in \Theta.
\]
The robust constrained control problem consists in designing a feedback law
\[
    u(k) = \kappa(x(k))
\]
such that the closed-loop system satisfies the constraints for all admissible uncertainty
realizations.\\
More precisely, the first requirement is robust constraint satisfaction:
\[
    x(k) \in \mathcal{X}, \qquad u(k) \in \mathcal{U},
    \qquad \forall k \geq 0,
\]
for all possible disturbance sequences. The second requirement is stability. Since persistent
disturbances may prevent convergence exactly to the origin, stability is usually interpreted as
convergence to a neighborhood of the origin, or as boundedness of the closed-loop trajectory in a
robust invariant region. The third requirement is performance. Depending on the available
information, one may optimize an expected cost, a worst-case cost, or a nominal cost. Finally,
one would like the controller to work from as large a set of initial states as possible.\\

These goals cannot be met without assumptions on the uncertainty. If the disturbance can be
arbitrarily large, no controller can guarantee bounded constraint satisfaction. Robust MPC
therefore requires a mathematical uncertainty model.

\subsection{Common uncertainty models}
\subsubsection{Examples of uncertainty models}
Several uncertainty models appear commonly in MPC. A first example is a constant measurement
or input bias. In this case, the dynamics may be written as
\[
    g(x(k),u(k),w(k);\theta)
    =
    \tilde g(x(k),u(k)) + \theta,
\]
where \(\theta\) is unknown but constant. Such a model is useful when the plant has a systematic
offset, for instance due to actuator bias or a constant disturbance.\\

A second important class is given by linear parameter-varying systems. In this case, the system
matrices depend on a parameter vector \(\theta(k)\), and the dynamics are written as
\[
    x(k+1)
    =
    \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_\theta} \theta_j(k) A_j \right)x(k)
    +
    \left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_\theta} \theta_j(k) B_j \right)u(k),
\]
where
\[
    \mathbf{1}^\top \theta(k) = 1,
    \qquad
    \theta(k) \geq 0.
\]
Thus the system matrices are convex combinations of a finite number of vertex systems. This
description is useful when the dynamics vary within a known polytope of models.\\

A third possibility is additive stochastic noise:
\[
    x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k) + w(k),
\]
where the probability distribution of \(w(k)\) is known. In that case, one can formulate stochastic
MPC problems, for example by minimizing expected cost or imposing chance constraints.\\
In this chapter we focus on the deterministic robust counterpart of this last model, namely
additive bounded noise.

\subsubsection{Additive bounded noise}

The uncertainty model considered in this chapter is
\[
    x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k) + w(k),
    \qquad
    w(k) \in \mathcal{W}.
\]
Here \(A\) and \(B\) are known, while the disturbance \(w(k)\) is unknown, time-varying, and
bounded inside the set \(\mathcal{W}\). The set \(\mathcal{W}\) is assumed to contain all possible
disturbance values that may affect the system.\\
This model is simple but very important. It captures the idea that the nominal linear dynamics
are correct up to an additive perturbation. It can also be used as a conservative approximation
of more complicated effects, such as small nonlinearities, discretization errors, or unmodeled
dynamics. The price paid for this simplicity is conservatism: if \(\mathcal{W}\) is chosen too large,
the resulting robust MPC controller may become overly cautious or even infeasible for many
initial states.\\

A crucial feature of this model is that the noise is persistent. The disturbance does not
necessarily become smaller over time, and therefore the uncertain trajectory need not approach
the nominal trajectory. Instead, the possible deviation between the real and nominal trajectories
accumulates through the dynamics.

\section{Tools: Robust Invariance, Set Difference and Set Sum}

\subsection{Robust invariance and robust pre-sets}
\subsubsection{Reminder: positive invariance}
Before introducing robust invariance, we recall the nominal notion of positive invariance. Consider
an autonomous system
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k)),
\]
or a closed-loop system obtained by applying a fixed feedback law,
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),\kappa(x(k))).
\]
A set \(\mathcal{O}\) is called positively invariant if
\[
    x(k) \in \mathcal{O}
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    x(k+1) \in \mathcal{O},
    \qquad \forall k \geq 0.
\]
Thus, once the state enters \(\mathcal{O}\), it remains in \(\mathcal{O}\) forever.\\
In MPC, invariant sets are useful because they provide terminal regions in which constraint
satisfaction can be guaranteed indefinitely. If \(\mathcal{X}_f \subseteq \mathcal{X}\) is invariant
under a local controller \(\kappa\), and if \(\kappa(x) \in \mathcal{U}\) for all
\(x \in \mathcal{X}_f\), then reaching \(\mathcal{X}_f\) at the end of the prediction horizon is
sufficient to guarantee that the constraints can be respected after the horizon.

\subsubsection{Robust positive invariant sets}

In the presence of disturbances, the autonomous dynamics take the form
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),w(k)),
    \qquad w(k) \in \mathcal{W}.
\]
The corresponding invariance notion must now hold for every possible disturbance.

\begin{theorembox}[Robust positive invariant set]
A set \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\) is called a robust positive invariant set for
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),w(k)), \qquad w(k) \in \mathcal{W},
\]
if
\[
    x \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    g(x,w) \in \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}
    \qquad \forall w \in \mathcal{W}.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The difference from nominal invariance is the universal quantifier over the disturbance. In the
nominal case, the next state must remain in the set under one deterministic successor. In the
robust case, every possible successor generated by the disturbance must remain in the set.\\
For a closed-loop system
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),\kappa(x(k)),w(k)),
\]
the definition is applied to the autonomous disturbed dynamics induced by the controller
\(\kappa\). A robust invariant terminal set therefore guarantees that, once the state reaches that
set, the controller can keep the state inside it despite all admissible disturbances.

\subsubsection{Robust pre-sets}

The robust pre-set is the backward-reachability object associated with robust invariance. Given a
target set \(\Omega\), the robust pre-set is the set of all states from which the next state belongs
to \(\Omega\) for every disturbance value:
\[
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
    :=
    \{x \mid g(x,w) \in \Omega \ \text{for all } w \in \mathcal{W}\}.
\]
Thus, \(x \in \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)\) means that no matter which admissible
disturbance occurs at the next step, the successor state is guaranteed to lie in \(\Omega\).\\
This definition is the robust counterpart of the nominal pre-set. The nominal pre-set only asks
whether the deterministic successor lies in the target set. The robust pre-set asks whether the
entire set of possible successors lies in the target set.

\subsubsection{Robust pre-sets for linear systems}

Consider now the linear disturbed system
\[
    x(k+1) = Ax(k) + w(k),
    \qquad w(k) \in \mathcal{W},
\]
and let the target set be the polyhedron
\[
    \Omega := \{x \in \R^n \mid Fx \leq f\}.
\]
The robust pre-set is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
    &=
    \{x \mid Ax+w \in \Omega,\ \forall w \in \mathcal{W}\}  \\
    &=
    \{x \mid F(Ax+w) \leq f,\ \forall w \in \mathcal{W}\}  \\
    &=
    \{x \mid FAx + Fw \leq f,\ \forall w \in \mathcal{W}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For each row \(F_i\) of \(F\), the condition becomes
\[
    F_iAx + F_iw \leq f_i
    \qquad \forall w \in \mathcal{W}.
\]
This is equivalent to requiring the inequality to hold for the worst possible disturbance in the
direction \(F_i\):
\[
    F_iAx \leq f_i - \max_{w \in \mathcal{W}} F_iw.
\]
Therefore,
\[
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega)
    =
    \left\{
    x \ \middle|\ 
    FAx \leq f - h_{\mathcal{W}}(F)
    \right\},
\]
where \(h_{\mathcal{W}}\) denotes the support function of \(\mathcal{W}\), applied row-wise:
\[
    h_{\mathcal{W}}(F)_i
    :=
    \max_{w \in \mathcal{W}} F_iw.
\]

This formula is one of the central algebraic tools of robust MPC. It shows that robust
constraint satisfaction can be converted into nominal constraint satisfaction with tightened
right-hand sides. The amount of tightening is determined by the support function of the
disturbance set.

\subsubsection{Geometric condition for robust invariance}

Robust invariance can be expressed through robust pre-sets exactly as in the nominal case.

\begin{theorembox}[Geometric condition for robust invariance]
A set \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\) is robust positively invariant for
\[
    x(k+1) = g(x(k),w(k)), \qquad w(k) \in \mathcal{W},
\]
if and only if
\[
    \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}
    \subseteq
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}).
\]
\end{theorembox}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{images/robust_invariance_geometric_condition.png}
    \caption{Geometric interpretation of the condition
    \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal W} \subseteq \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal W}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal W})\).
    The white region represents the set \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\), while the gray region
    represents its robust pre-set \(\operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal W}(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W})\).
    For a state \(x\) to belong to the robust pre-set, all possible successors
    \(g(x,w)\), for \(w\in\mathcal W\), must remain inside \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\).
    The orange set illustrates the collection of possible successor states generated by the
    admissible disturbances. Since this whole set lies inside \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\),
    the point \(x\) belongs to \(\operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal W}(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W})\).}
    \label{fig:robust_invariance_geometric_condition}
\end{figure}

The interpretation is direct. If every point of \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\) belongs to the robust
pre-set of \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\), then every point in the set is mapped back into the set
for all admissible disturbances. Figure~\ref{fig:robust_invariance_geometric_condition}
illustrates this idea geometrically: a state belongs to the robust pre-set if the entire set of
possible one-step successors remains inside \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\).

Conversely, if there exists a point in \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\) that is not in the robust
pre-set, then some admissible disturbance drives the successor outside \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}}\),
and the set is not robust invariant.

\subsubsection{Computing robust invariant sets}

The maximal robust invariant set can be computed conceptually through an iterative procedure.
Starting from the constraint set,
\[
    \Omega_0 := \mathcal{X},
\]
one recursively removes all states from which the next state cannot be guaranteed to remain in
the current candidate set:
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}
    :=
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega_i) \cap \Omega_i.
\]
The sequence is nested:
\[
    \Omega_{i+1} \subseteq \Omega_i.
\]
At each iteration, the set is made smaller by discarding states that may leave the current set
under some admissible disturbance. If the sequence reaches a fixed point,
\[
    \Omega_{i+1} = \Omega_i,
\]
then the algorithm has found a robust positively invariant set. In fact, under the usual
conditions, the fixed point is the maximal robust positively invariant subset of
\(\mathcal{X}\).

\begin{theorembox}[Conceptual algorithm for a robust invariant set]
Initialize
\[
    \Omega_0 \leftarrow \mathcal{X}.
\]
For \(i=0,1,2,\ldots\), compute
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}
    \leftarrow
    \operatorname{pre}_{\mathcal{W}}(\Omega_i) \cap \Omega_i.
\]
If
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}=\Omega_i,
\]
return
\[
    \mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W},\infty}=\Omega_i.
\]
\end{theorembox}

This is the same algorithm used in the nominal case, with the ordinary pre-set replaced by the
robust pre-set. The robust version is more conservative because a state is kept only if all
possible disturbed successors remain admissible.\\

The distinction between nominal and robust invariance is illustrated by the closed-loop disturbed
system
\[
    x(k+1) = (A+BK)x(k) + w(k),
\]
with
\[
    A =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 1 \\
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    B =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 \\
        0.5
    \end{bmatrix},
\]
and constraints
\[
    \mathcal{X}
    =
    \{x \mid \norm{x}_\infty \leq 5,\ \norm{Kx}_\infty \leq 1\},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{W}
    =
    \{w \mid \norm{w}_\infty \leq 0.3\}.
\]
Here \(K\) is chosen as an LQR controller. If one ignores the disturbance and computes the
maximal nominal invariant set, one obtains a region in which the nominal closed-loop trajectory
remains feasible. However, this set is not necessarily safe for the disturbed system. A state may
belong to the nominal invariant set and still be driven outside the constraints by an admissible
disturbance.

The maximal robust invariant set is therefore smaller. It contains only those states for which
the entire disturbance tube remains inside the constraints. This shrinking is not a defect of the
method; it is the price paid for a guarantee that holds for all disturbances in
\(\mathcal{W}\).

\subsection{Minkowski sum and Pontryagin difference}

To describe robust constraint tightening compactly, two set operations are especially useful:
the Minkowski sum and the Pontryagin difference.

\begin{theorembox}[Minkowski sum]
Let \(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B} \subseteq \R^n\). Their Minkowski sum is
\[
    \mathcal{A} \oplus \mathcal{B}
    :=
    \{x+y \mid x \in \mathcal{A},\ y \in \mathcal{B}\}.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The Minkowski sum describes all possible sums of one element from \(\mathcal{A}\) and one
element from \(\mathcal{B}\). In robust MPC, it naturally appears when adding the nominal state
to the possible disturbance offsets.

\begin{theorembox}[Pontryagin difference]
Let \(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B} \subseteq \R^n\). Their Pontryagin difference is
\[
    \mathcal{A} \ominus \mathcal{B}
    :=
    \{x \mid x+e \in \mathcal{A}\ \text{for all } e \in \mathcal{B}\}.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The Pontryagin difference is a constraint-tightening operation. If
\[
    x \in \mathcal{A} \ominus \mathcal{B},
\]
then adding any element of \(\mathcal{B}\) to \(x\) still keeps the result inside
\(\mathcal{A}\). Therefore, if \(\mathcal{B}\) represents the set of possible errors between the
real and nominal state, then imposing
\[
    x_{\mathrm{nom}} \in \mathcal{A} \ominus \mathcal{B}
\]
guarantees
\[
    x_{\mathrm{nom}} + e \in \mathcal{A}
    \qquad \forall e \in \mathcal{B}.
\]

\section{Impact of Bounded Additive Noise}

\subsection{Uncertain state evolution}
\subsubsection{Set-valued state evolution}
We now return to the uncertain linear system
\[
    x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k) + w(k),
    \qquad w(k) \in \mathcal{W}.
\]
Given the current state \(x(0)=x_0\) and a planned input sequence
\[
    U := \{u_0,u_1,\ldots,u_{N-1}\},
\]
the future state is not a single point, because it depends on the unknown disturbance sequence
\[
    W := \{w_0,w_1,\ldots,w_{N-1}\}.
\]
We denote by
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)
\]
the state reached at time \(i\) when starting from \(x_0\), applying the input sequence \(U\),
and observing the disturbance sequence \(W\).\\
For a fixed input sequence, each possible disturbance realization produces a different trajectory.
Thus, at time \(i\), the controller must reason about a set of possible states rather than a
single predicted state.

\subsubsection{Nominal and uncertain predictions}

For the nominal system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k),
\]
successive substitution gives
\[
    x_1 = Ax_0 + Bu_0,
\]
\[
    x_2 = A^2x_0 + ABu_0 + Bu_1,
\]
and, in general,
\[
    x_i
    =
    A^i x_0
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j B u_{i-1-j}.
\]
For the uncertain system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k),
\]
the first steps are
\[
    \phi_1 = Ax_0 + Bu_0 + w_0,
\]
\[
    \phi_2 = A^2x_0 + ABu_0 + Bu_1 + Aw_0 + w_1.
\]
Continuing in the same way yields
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)
    =
    A^i x_0
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j B u_{i-1-j}
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j w_{i-1-j}.
\]
Using the nominal prediction \(x_i\), this can be written compactly as
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)
    =
    x_i
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j w_{i-1-j}.
\]
Therefore, the uncertain state is equal to the nominal state plus an additive offset caused by
the accumulated disturbances.\\
This decomposition follows from linearity and is the key observation behind constraint
tightening. The nominal part depends on the chosen input sequence. The disturbance offset
depends only on the disturbance set and on the powers of \(A\).

\subsection{Robust constrained control under bounded additive noise}

\subsubsection{Goals under bounded additive noise}
We now return to the robust constrained control problem for the uncertain linear system
\[
    x(k+1) = Ax(k) + Bu(k) + w(k),
    \qquad
    x(k) \in \mathcal X,\quad u(k)\in\mathcal U,\quad w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
The objective is to design a feedback law
\[
    u(k)=\kappa(x(k))
\]
such that the closed-loop system satisfies the constraints for all possible disturbance
realizations, remains stable in an appropriate robust sense, and achieves good performance.\\
The essential difficulty is that, once the disturbance is present, the future state is no longer
known exactly. Even if the current state \(x_0\) and the future input sequence
\[
    U=\{u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}\}
\]
are fixed, different disturbance sequences
\[
    W=\{w_0,\ldots,w_{N-1}\}
\]
produce different future trajectories. Therefore, the controller cannot predict a single future
state at time \(i\), but only a set of possible states. The robust MPC design must then ensure
that all of these possible states remain inside the constraints.\\

The guiding idea is to construct a controller that satisfies constraints and stabilizes the system
for all admissible disturbances. In this chapter we do this by keeping the nominal prediction
model inside the MPC problem, but by tightening the constraints imposed on the nominal
trajectory. The tightening is chosen so that the real disturbed trajectory remains feasible even
though the optimizer only propagates the nominal dynamics.

\subsubsection{Defining a cost to minimize}

In nominal MPC, the finite-horizon cost assigns a value to a single predicted trajectory. For
example, for a planned input sequence \(U\), one writes
\[
    J(x_0,U)
    :=
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_i,u_i) + \ell_f(x_N),
\]
where \(x_i\) denotes the nominal state predicted from
\[
    x_{i+1}=Ax_i+Bu_i,
    \qquad x_0=x(0).
\]
In the uncertain case, however, the state at each time depends on the disturbance sequence.
Consequently, each possible disturbance realization produces a different trajectory and therefore
a different cost. The cost becomes
\[
    J(x_0,U,W)
    :=
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}
    \ell\bigl(\phi_i(x_0,U,W),u_i\bigr)
    +
    \ell_f\bigl(\phi_N(x_0,U,W)\bigr),
\]
where \(\phi_i(x_0,U,W)\) denotes the uncertain state reached at time \(i\).\\
To obtain an optimization problem that can be solved online, the dependence on the unknown
disturbance sequence \(W\) must be removed or summarized. There are several possible choices.
One may minimize the expected value
\[
    J_N(x_0,U)
    :=
    \mathbb E\left[J(x_0,U,W)\right],
\]
which requires probabilistic information about the disturbance. Alternatively, one may minimize
the worst-case cost
\[
    J_N(x_0,U)
    :=
    \max_{W\in\mathcal W^N} J(x_0,U,W),
\]
which leads to a min-max robust optimal control problem. A simpler possibility, and the one
used in this lecture, is to optimize the nominal cost
\[
    J_N(x_0,U)
    :=
    J(x_0,U,0).
\]
Thus, in the robust open-loop MPC formulation developed here, robustness is enforced through
the constraints, while the performance objective is evaluated on the nominal trajectory.

\subsubsection{Robust constraint satisfaction}

The main robust requirement is that the state and input constraints must be satisfied for every
admissible disturbance sequence. For the uncertain system
\[
    \phi_{i+1}=A\phi_i+Bu_i+w_i,
\]
this means that along the prediction horizon one would like to impose
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)\in\mathcal X,
    \qquad
    u_i\in\mathcal U,
    \qquad
    \forall W\in\mathcal W^N.
\]
The input constraint is straightforward in the open-loop formulation, because the planned input
sequence \(U\) is chosen directly by the optimizer and does not depend on the disturbance.
The state constraint is more difficult, because \(\phi_i\) is not a single point but a set of
possible states.\\
The key idea is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:robust_constraint_satisfaction}. Instead of requiring
only the nominal predicted state to lie in the original constraint set \(\mathcal X\), we require
the nominal state to lie in a tighter set. The tightening is chosen so that, after adding any
admissible disturbance effect, the real state remains inside the original constraint set.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/robust_constraint_satisfaction.png}
    \caption{Robust constraint satisfaction through constraint tightening. The nominal
    trajectory is required to satisfy tighter constraints so that the entire set of possible
    disturbed states remains inside the original constraint set \(\mathcal X\). At the terminal
    time, all possible terminal states must be contained in the robust terminal set
    \(\mathcal X_f\).}
    \label{fig:robust_constraint_satisfaction}
\end{figure}

This point of view separates the MPC prediction into two parts. Over the finite horizon
\(i=0,\ldots,N-1\), the optimizer chooses the input sequence
\[
    U=\{u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}\}
\]
and constraints are enforced explicitly on all possible disturbed predictions. At the end of the
horizon, one imposes a terminal constraint
\[
    \phi_N(x_0,U,W)\in\mathcal X_f
    \qquad
    \forall W\in\mathcal W^N,
\]
where \(\mathcal X_f\subseteq \mathcal X\) is chosen as a robust positively invariant set for the
post-horizon dynamics. In the simple open-loop construction considered here, after the horizon
one assumes no further planned input, i.e. \(u_i=0\) for \(i\geq N\), and therefore the
post-horizon system is
\[
    \phi_{i+1}=A\phi_i+w_i.
\]
If \(\mathcal X_f\) is robust positively invariant for this system and \(0\in\mathcal U\), then
once the terminal state is inside \(\mathcal X_f\), the constraints can be maintained
indefinitely despite the disturbance.

\subsubsection{Constraint tightening along the horizon}

We now derive the tightened constraints explicitly. From the linearity of the dynamics, the
uncertain state at prediction step \(i\) can be written as
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)
    =
    x_i
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j w_{i-1-j},
\]
where \(x_i\) is the nominal state obtained from
\[
    x_{i+1}=Ax_i+Bu_i,
    \qquad x_0=x(0).
\]
The goal is to ensure
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)\in\mathcal X
    \qquad
    \forall W\in\mathcal W^i.
\]
Equivalently, the set
\[
    \left\{
    x_i+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A^jw_{i-1-j}
    \ \middle|\
    w_0,\ldots,w_{i-1}\in\mathcal W
    \right\}
\]
must be contained in \(\mathcal X\).

Assume that the state constraint set is polyhedral,
\[
    \mathcal X=\{x\mid Fx\leq f\}.
\]
Then robust constraint satisfaction at prediction step \(i\) is equivalent to
\[
    F x_i
    +
    F\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A^j w_{i-1-j}
    \leq f
    \qquad
    \forall W\in\mathcal W^i.
\]
For each row of \(F\), we must protect against the largest possible disturbance contribution.
Thus,
\[
    F x_i
    \leq
    f
    -
    \max_{W\in\mathcal W^i}
    F\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A^j w_{i-1-j}.
\]
The maximization is understood row-wise. In terms of support functions, this can be written as
\[
    F x_i
    \leq
    f
    -
    h_{\mathcal W^i}
    \left(
        F\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A^j
    \right),
\]
or, more transparently, as a tightening by the set of all possible disturbance offsets. The
important conclusion is that robust satisfaction of the original constraint can be enforced by
imposing a tighter constraint on the nominal state \(x_i\). The support-function terms depend
only on \(A\), \(F\), and \(\mathcal W\), and can therefore be precomputed offline.

\begin{theorembox}[Constraint tightening]
For the disturbed linear system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k),
    \qquad w(k)\in\mathcal W,
\]
robust satisfaction of \(x_i\in\mathcal X\) can be enforced by requiring the nominal prediction
\(x_i\) to lie in a tightened constraint set. The tightening accounts for all disturbance effects
that can accumulate during the first \(i\) prediction steps.
\end{theorembox}

\subsubsection{Terminal state constraint}

The same argument applies to the terminal constraint. At the end of the horizon, we require not
only the nominal state \(x_N\), but every possible disturbed terminal state, to lie in the terminal
set:
\[
    \phi_N(x_0,U,W)\in\mathcal X_f
    \qquad
    \forall W\in\mathcal W^N.
\]
Using the same decomposition,
\[
    \phi_N(x_0,U,W)
    =
    x_N
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} A^j w_{N-1-j},
\]
this condition is enforced by tightening the terminal constraint in exactly the same way as the
intermediate state constraints. Hence the nominal terminal state must be chosen sufficiently
inside \(\mathcal X_f\) so that all possible disturbed terminal states remain in
\(\mathcal X_f\).

\subsubsection{Set notation and disturbance reachable sets}

The previous construction can be written compactly using Minkowski sums and the Pontryagin
difference. Define the disturbance reachable set at prediction step \(i\) as
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    :=
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1} A^j\mathcal W
    =
    \mathcal W\oplus A\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A^{i-1}\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0=\{0\}.
\]
This is the set of all possible deviations between the uncertain and nominal state after \(i\)
steps. Indeed,
\[
    \phi_i(x_0,U,W)
    \in
    x_i\oplus\mathcal F_i.
\]
Therefore, the condition that every possible disturbed state lies inside \(\mathcal X\) becomes
\[
    x_i\oplus\mathcal F_i\subseteq\mathcal X.
\]
By the definition of the Pontryagin difference, this is equivalent to
\[
    x_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i.
\]
Thus the robust state constraint can be written as
\[
    x_i
    \in
    \mathcal X
    \ominus
    \left(
        \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A^j\mathcal W
    \right).
\]
Similarly, the robust terminal condition is
\[
    x_N
    \in
    \mathcal X_f
    \ominus
    \mathcal F_N.
\]

The disturbance reachable sets satisfy the recursion
\[
    \mathcal F_{i+1}=A\mathcal F_i\oplus\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0=\{0\}.
\]
This recursion is useful in implementations, because the sets \(\mathcal F_i\), and therefore the
tightened constraints, can be computed offline before solving the MPC problem online.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/robust_constraint_tightening_sequence.png}
    \caption{Constraint tightening along the prediction horizon. Around each nominal predicted
    state \(x_i\), the disturbance reachable set describes the possible displacement of the real
    state. If the nominal state satisfies the tightened constraint
    \(x_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i\), then the whole uncertain set
    \(x_i\oplus\mathcal F_i\) remains inside the original constraint set \(\mathcal X\).}
    \label{fig:robust_constraint_tightening_sequence}
\end{figure}

\section{Robust Open-Loop MPC}

\subsection{Putting the ingredients together}

We can now combine the nominal cost, the nominal prediction dynamics, and the tightened
constraints. At time \(k\), robust open-loop MPC solves
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{U}\quad
    &
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(x_i,u_i)+\ell_f(x_N)
    \\
    \text{subj. to}\quad
    &
    x_{i+1}=Ax_i+Bu_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    x_0=x(k),
    \\
    &
    x_i\in
    \mathcal X
    \ominus
    \left(
        \mathcal W\oplus A\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A^{i-1}\mathcal W
    \right),
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    u_i\in\mathcal U,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    x_N\in
    \mathcal X_f
    \ominus
    \left(
        \mathcal W\oplus A\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A^{N-1}\mathcal W
    \right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Using the shorthand \(\mathcal F_i=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A^j\mathcal W\), this becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{U}\quad
    &
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(x_i,u_i)+\ell_f(x_N)
    \\
    \text{subj. to}\quad
    &
    x_{i+1}=Ax_i+Bu_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    x_0=x(k),
    \\
    &
    x_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i,
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    u_i\in\mathcal U,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    x_N\in\mathcal X_f\ominus\mathcal F_N.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(\mathcal X_f\subseteq\mathcal X\) is a robust positively invariant set for the autonomous
disturbed system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+w(k),
    \qquad w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Robust open-loop MPC]
Robust open-loop MPC solves a nominal finite-horizon optimal control problem, but imposes
tightened state and terminal constraints. If the nominal trajectory satisfies the tightened
constraints, then every disturbed trajectory generated by \(w(k)\in\mathcal W\) satisfies the
original constraints.
\end{theorembox}

The name ``open-loop'' refers to the structure of the prediction problem. The optimizer chooses
an input sequence
\[
    U=\{u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}\}
\]
without allowing the later inputs \(u_1,\ldots,u_{N-1}\) to depend on future disturbed states.
After solving the problem, MPC is still implemented in receding-horizon fashion:
\[
    u(k)=u_0^\star(x(k)).
\]
At the next sampling instant, the new state is measured and the optimization problem is solved
again.

\subsection{Interpretation and limitations}

The robust open-loop construction has a simple interpretation. We perform nominal MPC, but we
do not allow the nominal trajectory to approach the boundary of the true constraint set. Instead,
we keep a safety margin around it. This safety margin is exactly the disturbance reachable set
\(\mathcal F_i\). If the nominal state \(x_i\) lies in the tightened set
\(\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i\), then every possible disturbed state lies in the original set
\(\mathcal X\).\\

This approach gives a systematic way to guarantee robust constraint satisfaction, but it may be
conservative. The reason is that the planned future inputs are open-loop: they cannot react to
the disturbances that will be observed in the future. The controller does re-optimize at the next
sampling instant, so feedback exists at the closed-loop level, but the prediction problem itself
does not optimize over feedback policies.\\
As a result, robust open-loop MPC can have a small feasible region, or region of attraction,
especially when the disturbance set \(\mathcal W\) is large or the horizon is long. More advanced
robust MPC schemes reduce this conservatism by explicitly introducing feedback in the prediction
model, for example through tube-based MPC. The tools developed in this chapter, namely robust
invariance, disturbance reachable sets, Pontryagin difference, and constraint tightening, are the
basic ingredients for those methods.

\section{Closed-Loop Predictions}

\subsection{Why open-loop robust predictions are conservative}

The robust open-loop MPC formulation introduced in the previous section already provides a
systematic way to guarantee constraint satisfaction under bounded additive disturbances.
However, it does so by optimizing over a fixed future input sequence
\[
    U=\{u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}\}.
\]
This means that, inside the prediction problem, the controller commits today to all future
control moves. The future inputs are not allowed to depend on the disturbances that will be
observed along the horizon.\\

This assumption is conservative. In reality, MPC is implemented in closed loop: after applying
the first input, the state is measured again and the optimization is solved again. Therefore, if a
disturbance pushes the system away from the predicted trajectory, the controller will not blindly
continue with the old plan. It will react to the new state. The open-loop robust prediction does
not fully exploit this future feedback capability.\\
A useful way to understand this point is to view robust MPC as a game between two players:
the controller and the disturbance. At each time step, the controller selects an input and the
disturbance selects an admissible perturbation. For the uncertain system
\[
    x(k+1)=g(x(k),u(k))+w(k),
\]
the true sequence of events is the following. The controller first applies \(u(k)\), the disturbance
then realizes \(w(k)\), the new state is observed, and the controller chooses the next input based
on this new state.\\

In an open-loop robust prediction, instead, the controller chooses all future moves
\[
    u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}
\]
at once, while the disturbance is allowed to choose an entire disturbance sequence after seeing
the whole planned input sequence. In other words, the prediction assumes that the controller
will do the same thing in the future no matter what the disturbance does. This is safe, but it
does not reflect the way MPC actually uses feedback.

\subsection{Control policies}

A less conservative robust prediction should allow the future control moves to depend on the
future states. Instead of optimizing over a sequence
\[
    \{u_0,u_1,\ldots,u_{N-1}\},
\]
one would like to optimize over a sequence of feedback policies
\[
    \{u_0,\mu_1(\cdot),\ldots,\mu_{N-1}(\cdot)\},
\]
where
\[
    \mu_i:\R^n\to\R^m
\]
maps the predicted state at time \(i\) to the input to be applied at time \(i\).\\

The first input is still a single value, because the current state is known at the time the
optimization problem is solved. The later inputs, however, should be functions. For instance,
after the first disturbance \(w_0\) has occurred, the state
\[
    x_1=Ax_0+Bu_0+w_0
\]
is known, and the second input can be chosen as
\[
    u_1=\mu_1(x_1).
\]
Equivalently, since \(x_1\) is determined by the previous disturbance, one may regard \(u_1\) as
a function of \(w_0\). At the next step, \(u_2\) may depend on \(w_0\) and \(w_1\), and so on.\\
This idea captures the actual information pattern of closed-loop control. The difficulty is that
one cannot optimize over arbitrary functions online. The set of all possible nonlinear policies is
infinite-dimensional and far too complex for a tractable MPC formulation. Therefore, practical
robust MPC methods impose a structure on the future policies.

\subsection{Structured closed-loop predictions}

Several structured policy classes can be used to introduce feedback into the prediction problem.
A first possibility is to use a fixed \textbf{pre-stabilizing} feedback of the form
\[
    \mu_i(x_i)=Kx_i+v_i,
\]
where \(K\) is chosen offline such that \(A+BK\) is stable, and the optimizer only chooses the
feedforward correction \(v_i\). This is simple and leads to a convex problem, but it can be
conservative.\\

A more general possibility is to optimize over \text{linear feedback} gains,
\[
    \mu_i(x_i)=K_i x_i+v_i.
\]
This gives more freedom, but the resulting optimization problem is generally nonconvex and
therefore very difficult to solve online.\\

Another important idea is \textbf{disturbance feedback}. In this case the future input is written as an
affine function of the past disturbances:
\[
    \mu_i
    =
    v_i+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}M_{ij}w_j.
\]
Although this is equivalent in expressive power to a class of linear state-feedback policies, it can
lead to convex formulations. The drawback is that the number of decision variables may become
large, making the method computationally demanding.\\

The approach emphasized in this chapter is \textbf{tube MPC}. The future input is decomposed as
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
\]
where \(z_i\) is a nominal state, \(v_i\) is a nominal input, and \(K\) is a fixed stabilizing
feedback gain. The optimizer chooses the nominal trajectory and the nominal input sequence,
while the feedback term \(K(x_i-z_i)\) keeps the real trajectory close to the nominal one.

\section{Error Dynamics}

\subsection{Splitting nominal planning and error correction}

We consider again the disturbed linear system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k),
    \qquad
    x(k)\in\mathcal X,\quad u(k)\in\mathcal U,\quad w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
The central idea is to split the available control authority into two parts. The first part plans
a nominal trajectory for the noise-free system
\[
    z(k+1)=Az(k)+Bv(k).
\]
Here \(z(k)\) is the nominal state and \(v(k)\) is the nominal input. The second part is a local
tracking controller that compensates for deviations between the real state and the nominal
state. We write
\[
    u(k)=v(k)+K(x(k)-z(k)),
\]
where \(K\) is chosen offline so that
\[
    A_K:=A+BK
\]
is Schur stable.\\

The optimization problem will choose the nominal trajectory and the nominal inputs. The
feedback gain \(K\) is not optimized online. It is used to keep the real trajectory inside a tube
around the nominal trajectory. This is what makes the resulting MPC problem tractable: the
online optimization remains a nominal finite-horizon optimal control problem, but with tightened
constraints that account for the possible tracking error.

\subsection{Derivation of the error dynamics}

Define the error between the real and nominal state as
\[
    e_i:=x_i-z_i.
\]
Using the feedback law
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i)=v_i+Ke_i,
\]
and the nominal dynamics
\[
    z_{i+1}=Az_i+Bv_i,
\]
we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
    e_{i+1}
    &=
    x_{i+1}-z_{i+1}                                                    \\
    &=
    \left(Ax_i+Bu_i+w_i\right)-\left(Az_i+Bv_i\right)                  \\
    &=
    Ax_i+B\left(v_i+K(x_i-z_i)\right)+w_i-Az_i-Bv_i                    \\
    &=
    (A+BK)(x_i-z_i)+w_i                                                \\
    &=
    A_K e_i+w_i.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus the error evolves according to the autonomous disturbed system
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_K e_i+w_i,
    \qquad w_i\in\mathcal W.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Error dynamics under tube feedback]
If
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
    \qquad
    z_{i+1}=Az_i+Bv_i,
\]
then the error \(e_i=x_i-z_i\) satisfies
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_K e_i+w_i,
    \qquad A_K=A+BK.
\]
The nominal input \(v_i\) disappears from the error dynamics.
\end{theorembox}

This cancellation is fundamental. It means that the possible tracking errors are determined only
by the disturbance set \(\mathcal W\) and by the closed-loop matrix \(A_K\), not by the particular
nominal trajectory chosen online. Hence the error bounds can be computed offline.\\
Furthermore, the dynamics $A+BK$ are stable, and the set $\mathcal W$ is bounded, so there is some set $\mathcal E$ that $e$ will stay inside for all time.\\
We wish now to bound the maximum error, or to see how far the real trajector is from the nominal trajectory.

\subsection{Uncertain state evolution of the error}

We now study more carefully how the error evolves under the feedback law introduced above. The
error dynamics are
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_K e_i+w_i,
    \qquad
    A_K:=A+BK,
    \qquad
    w_i\in\mathcal W.
\]
Assume first that the real and nominal trajectories coincide at the beginning of the prediction,
so that
\[
    e_0=0.
\]
This is the situation that occurs, for instance, when the nominal prediction is initialized at the
current measured state. The first prediction steps are then
\[
    e_1=w_0,
\]
\[
    e_2=A_K e_1+w_1=A_Kw_0+w_1,
\]
and, by repeated substitution,
\[
    e_i
    =
    \sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j w_{i-1-j}.
\]
This formula shows explicitly how the disturbances accumulate through the stable dynamics
\(A_K\). The error at time \(i\) is not produced only by the last disturbance \(w_{i-1}\), but by
all previous disturbances, each propagated forward by the appropriate power of \(A_K\).\\
Since each disturbance satisfies
\[
    w_i\in\mathcal W,
\]
we can ask the following set-valued question: where can the error be at prediction step \(i\)?
The answer is the disturbance reachable set
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    =
    \mathcal W\oplus A_K\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A_K^{i-1}\mathcal W
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0:=\{0\}.
\]
Thus
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal F_i
\]
for every disturbance sequence \(w_0,\ldots,w_{i-1}\in\mathcal W\). The set \(\mathcal F_i\)
therefore contains all possible errors at time \(i\), assuming the prediction starts from zero
error.

\begin{theorembox}[Disturbance reachable set]
For the error dynamics
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_Ke_i+w_i,
    \qquad w_i\in\mathcal W,
\]
with initial condition \(e_0=0\), the set of all possible errors at prediction step \(i\) is
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0=\{0\}.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The notation used above is the Minkowski sum:
\[
    \mathcal P\oplus\mathcal Q
    :=
    \{x+y\mid x\in\mathcal P,\ y\in\mathcal Q\}.
\]
Hence \(\mathcal F_i\) is obtained by adding all disturbance sets after propagation through the
closed-loop matrix \(A_K\).

\subsection{Minimum robust invariant set}

As the prediction step \(i\) increases, the disturbance reachable set contains more and more
terms:
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    =
    \mathcal W\oplus A_K\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A_K^{i-1}\mathcal W.
\]
If \(A_K\) is Schur stable and \(\mathcal W\) is bounded, these accumulated disturbance effects
remain bounded. In the limit, one obtains
\[
    \mathcal F_\infty
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0:=\{0\}.
\]
This limiting set is the minimum robust positively invariant set associated with the error
dynamics
\[
    e^+=A_Ke+w.
\]
It is ``minimum'' in the sense that it is the smallest set that necessarily contains all possible
steady-state error values generated by the persistent disturbance.\\
The invariance property can be seen directly from the definition. If
\[
    e\in\mathcal F_\infty,
\]
then, after one disturbed step,
\[
    e^+=A_Ke+w\in A_K\mathcal F_\infty\oplus\mathcal W.
\]
But
\[
\begin{aligned}
    A_K\mathcal F_\infty\oplus\mathcal W
    &=
    A_K\left(
    \mathcal W\oplus A_K\mathcal W\oplus A_K^2\mathcal W\oplus\cdots
    \right)
    \oplus\mathcal W                                      \\
    &=
    A_K\mathcal W\oplus A_K^2\mathcal W\oplus A_K^3\mathcal W\oplus\cdots
    \oplus\mathcal W                                      \\
    &=
    \mathcal W\oplus A_K\mathcal W\oplus A_K^2\mathcal W\oplus\cdots       \\
    &=
    \mathcal F_\infty.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore,
\[
    e\in\mathcal F_\infty
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    A_Ke+w\in\mathcal F_\infty
    \qquad
    \forall w\in\mathcal W.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Minimum robust positively invariant set]
For the error dynamics
\[
    e^+=A_Ke+w,
    \qquad w\in\mathcal W,
\]
with \(A_K\) Schur stable, the set
\[
    \mathcal F_\infty
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}A_K^j\mathcal W
\]
is the minimum robust positively invariant set generated by the disturbance.
\end{theorembox}

A conceptual algorithm for computing this set is obtained by accumulating the terms in the
Minkowski sum:
\[
    \Omega_0\leftarrow\{0\},
\]
and then, for \(i=0,1,2,\ldots\),
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}\leftarrow \Omega_i\oplus A_K^i\mathcal W.
\]
If there exists a finite index \(n\) such that
\[
    \mathcal F_n=\mathcal F_{n+1},
\]
then no new error directions are generated by further disturbances and
\[
    \mathcal F_n=\mathcal F_\infty.
\]
In general, such a finite \(n\) does not always exist. In that case, a sufficiently large \(n\)
gives an approximation, or one computes a robust invariant outer approximation that is slightly
larger than \(\mathcal F_\infty\).

\begin{theorembox}[Conceptual computation of \(\mathcal F_\infty\)]
Initialize
\[
    \Omega_0\leftarrow\{0\}.
\]
For \(i=0,1,2,\ldots\), compute
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}\leftarrow \Omega_i\oplus A_K^i\mathcal W.
\]
If
\[
    \Omega_{i+1}=\Omega_i,
\]
then return
\[
    \mathcal F_\infty=\Omega_i.
\]
If finite termination does not occur, one uses a finite approximation or an invariant outer
approximation.
\end{theorembox}

\subsection{Example: convergence to the minimum robust invariant set}

The construction can be illustrated on the closed-loop disturbed system
\[
    x(k+1)
    =
    \left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 1\\
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1\\
        0.5
    \end{bmatrix}
    K
    \right)x(k)
    +
    w(k),
\]
with disturbance set
\[
    \mathcal W
    :=
    \{w\mid |w_1|\leq 0.01,\ |w_2|\leq 0.1\}.
\]
Here \(K\) is chosen as the LQR controller for
\[
    Q=I,
    \qquad
    R=10.
\]
The sets
\[
    A_K^j\mathcal W
\]
are propagated disturbance sets. Their Minkowski sum grows toward the limiting set
\[
    \mathcal F_\infty.
\]
Once the error is inside this set, every subsequent disturbed evolution remains inside the same
set. Therefore, the state trajectory of the error system stays in \(\mathcal F_\infty\) for all
time.\\

    \label{fig:minimum_robust_invariant_set_example}

This example emphasizes the role of the stabilizing feedback \(K\). The disturbance is persistent,
so the error cannot be forced to zero. However, if \(A_K\) is stable, the effect of old
disturbances decays, and the total error remains confined to a bounded robust invariant set.

\subsection{From error bounds to robust MPC: two variants}

The error dynamics
\[
    e_{i+1}=(A+BK)e_i+w_i=A_Ke_i+w_i,
    \qquad
    w_i\in\mathcal W,
\]
provide the basis for two robust MPC constructions. Both use the same feedback gain \(K\), and
both plan a nominal trajectory. The difference is in how the error between the real and nominal
states is bounded.\\

The first possibility is to use the disturbance reachable sets
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    e_0=0.
\]
In this case the error bound depends on the prediction step. At the beginning of the prediction
the error is zero, while later along the horizon the set \(\mathcal F_i\) becomes larger as more
disturbances may have accumulated. This leads to robust constraint-tightening MPC. The tube
around the nominal trajectory is time-varying:
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i.
\]

The second possibility is to use a robust positively invariant set \(\mathcal E\), satisfying
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    e_{i+1}\in\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall w_i\in\mathcal W.
\]
Then, for any initial error \(e_0\in\mathcal E\), one has
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall i\geq 0.
\]
This leads to tube MPC. Instead of a growing tube, one uses a constant tube cross-section:
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal E.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Two robust MPC variants from the error dynamics]
The error dynamics can be used in two main ways:
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal F_i
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad e_0=0,
\]
which leads to robust constraint-tightening MPC, or
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \mathcal E \ \text{RPI for } e^+=A_Ke+w,
\]
which leads to tube MPC.
\end{theorembox}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    % Replace the file name with the corresponding slide/image file.
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{images/error_bounds_two_variants.png}
    \caption{From error bounds to robust MPC. On the left, disturbance reachable sets
    \(\mathcal F_i\) give a time-varying tube and lead to robust constraint-tightening MPC.
    On the right, a robust positively invariant set \(\mathcal E\) gives a constant tube and
    leads to tube MPC.}
    \label{fig:error_bounds_two_variants}
\end{figure}

The disturbance-reachable-set approach is naturally tied to predictions initialized with zero
error. It can be less conservative near the beginning of the horizon, because
\[
    \mathcal F_0=\{0\}.
\]
The RPI-set approach is more uniform: it assumes that the initial error may already lie anywhere
inside \(\mathcal E\), and then guarantees that it remains there forever. This is the viewpoint
used in tube MPC.
\section{Robust Constraint-Tightening MPC with Error Feedback}

\subsection{Noisy trajectory around a nominal trajectory}

We now use the error dynamics to obtain a less conservative version of constraint tightening
than the open-loop method discussed before. The real and nominal trajectories are related by
\[
    x_i=z_i+e_i.
\]
If the prediction starts from
\[
    z_0=x(k),
\]
then
\[
    e_0=x(k)-z_0=0.
\]
Therefore, under the feedback law
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
\]
the error satisfies
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal F_i.
\]
Consequently,
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i.
\]
The nominal state \(z_i\) is the center of a possible set of real states. The set
\(\mathcal F_i\) describes the maximum possible deviation from this center at prediction step
\(i\).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    % Replace the file name with the corresponding slide/image file.
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/noisy_trajectory_drs.png}
    \caption{At prediction step \(i\), the real state satisfies
    \(x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i\). The nominal state \(z_i\) is the planned tube center, while
    \(\mathcal F_i\) bounds the possible deviation caused by disturbances.}
    \label{fig:noisy_trajectory_drs}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Tightened state and input constraints}

The robust state constraint requires
\[
    x_i\in\mathcal X
    \qquad
    \forall w_0,\ldots,w_{i-1}\in\mathcal W.
\]
Since
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i,
\]
this is guaranteed if and only if
\[
    z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i\subseteq\mathcal X.
\]
By the definition of the Pontryagin difference, this is equivalent to
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i.
\]
Thus, instead of imposing the original state constraint on the nominal trajectory, we impose the
tightened constraint
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i.
\]
The same reasoning applies to the input. Since
\[
    u_i=v_i+Ke_i
\]
and \(e_i\in\mathcal F_i\), the set of possible inputs at time \(i\) is
\[
    u_i\in v_i\oplus K\mathcal F_i.
\]
Robust satisfaction of the input constraint is therefore guaranteed by
\[
    v_i\oplus K\mathcal F_i\subseteq\mathcal U,
\]
or equivalently
\[
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Constraint tightening with disturbance reachable sets]
If
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal F_i,
\]
then robust satisfaction of the original constraints
\[
    x_i\in\mathcal X,\qquad u_i\in\mathcal U
\]
is guaranteed by imposing the nominal constraints
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i,
    \qquad
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The sets \(\mathcal F_i\) depend only on \(A_K\) and \(\mathcal W\). Hence the tightened sets
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_i,
    \qquad
    \mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i
\]
can be computed offline.

\subsection{Robust constraint-tightening MPC problem}

We now combine the nominal system dynamics with the disturbance reachable sets derived above.
The idea is to optimize a nominal trajectory
\[
    z_0,z_1,\ldots,z_N
\]
and a nominal input sequence
\[
    v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_{N-1},
\]
while tightening the state and input constraints so that the real disturbed trajectory remains
feasible for all admissible disturbances.\\

Recall that the error dynamics are governed by
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_Ke_i+w_i,
    \qquad
    A_K:=A+BK,
\]
and that, when the prediction starts from zero error, the error at step \(i\) belongs to the
disturbance reachable set
\[
    \mathcal F_i
    :=
    \mathcal W\oplus A_K\mathcal W\oplus\cdots\oplus A_K^{i-1}\mathcal W
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad
    \mathcal F_0:=\{0\}.
\]
Therefore, if the nominal state is \(z_i\), then the real state can lie anywhere in
\[
    z_i\oplus \mathcal F_i.
\]
Likewise, since the control law is
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
\]
the real input lies in
\[
    v_i\oplus K\mathcal F_i.
\]
Robust satisfaction of the original constraints is then enforced by requiring
\[
    z_i\in \mathcal X\ominus \mathcal F_i,
    \qquad
    v_i\in \mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i.
\]

At time \(k\), robust constraint-tightening MPC solves the nominal optimization problem
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{Z,V}\quad
    &
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(z_i,v_i)+\ell_f(z_N)
    \\
    \text{subj. to}\quad
    &
    z_{i+1}=Az_i+Bv_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus \mathcal F_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    z_N\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\ominus \mathcal F_N,
    \\
    &
    z_0=x(k).
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Robust constraint-tightening MPC problem]
The robust constraint-tightening MPC problem optimizes the nominal dynamics
\[
    z_{i+1}=Az_i+Bv_i
\]
subject to the tightened constraints
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus \mathcal F_i,
    \qquad
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal F_i,
\]
and the tightened terminal constraint
\[
    z_N\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\ominus \mathcal F_N.
\]
Here
\[
    \mathcal F_i=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}A_K^j\mathcal W,
    \qquad \mathcal F_0=\{0\},
\]
and the prediction is initialized with
\[
    z_0=x(k).
\]
\end{theorembox}

The initialization
\[
    z_0=x(k)
\]
is important. It implies that the initial error is zero, and therefore the first applied input is
simply
\[
    u(k)=v_0^\star+K\bigl(x(k)-z_0^\star\bigr)=v_0^\star.
\]
Thus the feedback term does not affect the first move, but it is essential in the prediction of the
future error bounds through the sets \(\mathcal F_i\). The terminal set
\(\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\) is chosen so as to be robustly invariant under the terminal
controller.

\subsection{Properties of robust constraint-tightening MPC}

The main property of this formulation is recursive feasibility. Intuitively, if the optimization
problem is feasible at the current state, then after one disturbed step it remains feasible at the
next state as well. This is the robust counterpart of the standard feasibility result in nominal
MPC.\\

To guarantee this property, the terminal ingredients \((\ell_f,\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}})\) must
be chosen so that the terminal set is compatible with the terminal controller. More precisely,
we assume
\[
    \mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\subseteq \mathcal X,
\]
and that for every
\[
    z\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}},
\]
the corresponding terminal input satisfies
\[
    Kz\in\mathcal U,
\]
while the terminal set is robust positively invariant under the disturbed closed-loop dynamics,
that is,
\[
    (A+BK)z+w\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}
    \qquad
    \forall w\in\mathcal W.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Robust invariance of the terminal set]
Suppose the terminal ingredients \((\ell_f,\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}})\) are designed such that
\[
    \mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\subseteq \mathcal X,
\]
and for all \(z\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\),
\[
    Kz\in\mathcal U,
\]
\[
    (A+BK)z+w\in\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}
    \qquad
    \forall w\in\mathcal W.
\]
Then \(\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\) is a robust positively invariant terminal set for the
terminal controller.
\end{theorembox}

Let \(\mathcal X_N\) denote the feasible set of the robust constraint-tightening MPC problem,
and let \(V^\star(x(k))\) denote the corresponding optimizer for the state \(x(k)\in\mathcal X_N\).
Then, after applying the first optimal nominal input \(v_0^\star(x(k))\), the next real state is
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bv_0^\star(x(k))+w(k).
\]
Since the optimization problem was formulated so that the predicted tube is feasible for every
possible disturbance realization, it is in particular feasible for the disturbance that is actually
observed. Hence
\[
    x(k+1)\in\mathcal X_N
    \qquad
    \forall w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Recursive feasibility]
Let \(\mathcal X_N\) be the feasible set of the robust constraint-tightening MPC problem.
Under the robust terminal invariance assumptions above, if
\[
    x(k)\in\mathcal X_N,
\]
then
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bv_0^\star(x(k))+w(k)\in\mathcal X_N
    \qquad
    \forall w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
Therefore the robust constraint-tightening MPC problem is recursively feasible.
\end{theorembox}

This result is the essential robustness guarantee of the scheme. It says that once the problem is
feasible, feasibility is preserved for all future times despite the disturbance. Stability analysis,
however, requires a more refined notion, typically input-to-state stability, and goes beyond the
basic recursive-feasibility argument.

\subsection{Intuition}

The geometric interpretation of the method is very useful. Around each nominal prediction
\(z_i\), one considers the disturbance reachable set \(\mathcal F_i\). Hence the real state at step
\(i\) may lie anywhere in the set
\[
    z_i\oplus \mathcal F_i.
\]
If this whole set remains inside the state constraints, then the real state is guaranteed to satisfy
the original constraints for every admissible disturbance realization.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/constraint_tightening_intuition_1.png}
    \caption{Intuition behind robust constraint-tightening MPC. Around each nominal state
    \(z_i\), the disturbance reachable set \(\mathcal F_i\) describes the possible location of the
    real state \(x_i\). The nominal trajectory must therefore remain sufficiently far from the
    boundary of the constraint set.}
    \label{fig:constraint_tightening_intuition_1}
\end{figure}

After one control step, the actual disturbed state is not exactly at the nominal successor
\(z_1^\star\), but rather at
\[
    x^+=z_1^\star+w,
\]
or, more generally, inside the corresponding disturbed tube. The candidate nominal trajectory at
the next time step is then obtained by shifting the previous optimal trajectory forward and
accounting for the effect of the disturbance. The key geometric fact is that the disturbance
reachable set around this shifted candidate remains contained in the previously feasible tube.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/constraint_tightening_intuition_2.png}
    \caption{After the first disturbance is realized, the next nominal candidate is perturbed with
    respect to the previous optimal nominal trajectory. The new disturbance reachable tube is
    contained in the previously feasible one, which is the key idea behind recursive feasibility.}
    \label{fig:constraint_tightening_intuition_2}
\end{figure}

This picture explains why the formulation is less conservative than purely open-loop robust MPC.
The future tube is not built only from the open-loop system matrix \(A\), but from the stabilized
error dynamics
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_Ke_i+w_i,
\]
so the feedback correction \(K\) already enters the prediction through the sets \(\mathcal F_i\).

\subsection{Proof sketch}

We now sketch the recursive-feasibility argument more explicitly. Let
\[
    \{z_0^\star,\ldots,z_N^\star\},
    \qquad
    \{v_0^\star,\ldots,v_{N-1}^\star\}
\]
be the optimal nominal state and input sequences at time \(k\). Because the problem is
initialized with
\[
    z_0^\star=x(k),
\]
the first applied control is
\[
    u(k)=v_0^\star.
\]
After the disturbance \(w(k)\) is realized, the next state is
\[
    x^+=x(k+1)=z_1^\star+w(k).
\]

To prove recursive feasibility, we construct a candidate solution for the optimization problem at
time \(k+1\). As in the slides, this candidate is based on the error feedback and on the terminal
controller:
\[
    v_i=v_{i+1}^\star+K(z_i-z_{i+1}^\star),
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
\]
with
\[
    v_N^\star=Kz_N^\star,
    \qquad
    z_{N+1}^\star=A_K z_N^\star.
\]
Recursive application yields the candidate state and input sequences
\[
    z_i=z_{i+1}^\star+A_K^i w(k),
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N,
\]
and
\[
    v_i=v_{i+1}^\star+KA_K^i w(k),
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1.
\]
One verifies by induction that the candidate satisfies the nominal dynamics. Indeed,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    z_{i+1}
    &=Az_i+Bv_i \\
    &=A\bigl(z_{i+1}^\star+A_K^i w(k)\bigr)
      +B\Bigl(v_{i+1}^\star+K\bigl(z_i-z_{i+1}^\star\bigr)\Bigr) \\
    &=z_{i+2}^\star+A_K^{i+1}w(k).
\end{aligned}
\]
This matches exactly the recursive construction above.

The next step is the nested-tube argument. For each prediction step,
\[
\begin{aligned}
    z_i\oplus \mathcal F_i
    &=
    z_{i+1}^\star+A_K^i w(k)\oplus \mathcal F_i \\
    &\subseteq
    z_{i+1}^\star\oplus A_K^i\mathcal W\oplus \mathcal F_i \\
    &=
    z_{i+1}^\star\oplus \mathcal F_{i+1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Hence the candidate prediction tube at time \(k+1\) is contained in the previously feasible tube
at time \(k\). This proves that all tightened state constraints remain satisfied. The input
constraints are shown in exactly the same way.

At the terminal step, one obtains
\[
\begin{aligned}
    z_N\oplus \mathcal F_N
    &\subseteq
    z_{N+1}^\star\oplus \mathcal F_{N+1} \\
    &=
    A_K\bigl(z_N^\star\oplus \mathcal F_N\bigr)\oplus \mathcal W \\
    &\subseteq
    A_K\mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}}\oplus \mathcal W \\
    &\subseteq
    \mathcal X_f^{\mathrm{ct}},
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last inclusion follows from robust positive invariance of the terminal set. Therefore
the terminal constraint is also satisfied. This completes the proof sketch: the shifted candidate is
feasible, and so the optimization problem remains feasible at the next step.

\begin{theorembox}[Idea of the proof]
The recursive-feasibility proof is based on three ingredients:
the shifted nominal candidate, the containment
\[
    z_i\oplus\mathcal F_i\subseteq z_{i+1}^\star\oplus\mathcal F_{i+1},
\]
and robust invariance of the terminal set. Together, these show that the candidate built at time
\(k+1\) satisfies all tightened constraints.
\end{theorembox}
\section{Robust Tube MPC}

\subsection{Idea and construction}

\subsubsection{Tube MPC: the idea}

The robust constraint-tightening MPC formulation used disturbance reachable sets
\(\mathcal F_i\), which depend on the prediction step. Tube MPC takes a slightly different
viewpoint. Instead of using a different error set at every step, it bounds the error by a single
robust positively invariant set \(\mathcal E\).\\

The nominal system is still planned without disturbances:
\[
    z(k+1)=Az(k)+Bv(k).
\]
The real system is affected by the disturbance:
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k),
    \qquad w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
The controller is assumed to have the form
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
\]
where \(K\) is a fixed stabilizing feedback gain. The nominal input \(v_i\) steers the nominal
trajectory, while the feedback term \(K(x_i-z_i)\) keeps the real trajectory close to the nominal
one.\\
The error
\[
    e_i=x_i-z_i
\]
then satisfies
\[
    e_{i+1}=A_Ke_i+w_i,
    \qquad
    A_K:=A+BK.
\]
If \(\mathcal E\) is a robust positively invariant set for this error dynamics, then
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    e_{i+1}\in\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall w_i\in\mathcal W.
\]
Therefore, once the error lies in \(\mathcal E\), it remains in \(\mathcal E\) for all future times.
As a consequence, the real state remains inside the tube
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal E.
\]

    \centering

    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.45\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/tube_mpc_idea_nominal.png}
        \caption{Nominal trajectory with tube cross-sections.The real trajectory stays close to the nominal one.}
        \label{fig:tube_mpc_idea_nominal}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.45\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/tube_mpc_idea_constraints.png}
        \caption{The whole tube must satisfy the state constraints.}
        \label{fig:tube_mpc_idea_constraints}
    \end{subfigure}

    \caption{Tube MPC idea. The optimizer plans a nominal trajectory \(z_i\), while the feedback
    law \(u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i)\) keeps the real state \(x_i\) inside the tube
    \(z_i\oplus\mathcal E\). The tube must remain inside the admissible state constraint set.}
    \label{fig:tube_mpc_idea_three_steps}
\end{figure}

In this sense, tube MPC ignores the noise when planning the nominal trajectory, but accounts for
the noise by surrounding the nominal trajectory with an invariant tube. Ideally, one chooses
\[
    \mathcal E=\mathcal F_\infty
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}A_K^j\mathcal W,
\]
namely the minimal robust positively invariant set of the error dynamics. In practice,
\(\mathcal F_\infty\) may be difficult to compute exactly, and one often uses a robust invariant
outer approximation.

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC idea]
Tube MPC plans a nominal trajectory \(z_i\) for the disturbance-free system and applies the
feedback law
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i).
\]
If the error set \(\mathcal E\) is robust positively invariant for
\[
    e^+=A_Ke+w,
\]
then
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal E
\]
for all admissible disturbance realizations.
\end{theorembox}

\subsubsection{What must be designed}

The construction of tube MPC consists of two parts. First, one must build the tube and the
corresponding tightened nominal constraints. Second, one must prove that the resulting
closed-loop controller has the desired robust properties.\\

The design therefore requires the following ingredients. One must compute a set
\(\mathcal E\) such that the error remains inside it. Then one must modify the constraints on the
nominal trajectory \(z_i\) so that the entire tube \(z_i\oplus\mathcal E\) remains feasible.
Finally, one must formulate the resulting problem as a tractable optimization problem.\\

After the formulation is defined, two properties have to be established. The first is robust
constraint satisfaction: for all admissible disturbances, the real state and input must satisfy
\[
    x_i\in\mathcal X,
    \qquad
    u_i\in\mathcal U.
\]
The second is robust stability: because the disturbance is persistent, the state cannot generally
converge exactly to the origin, but it should converge to the invariant tube around the origin.

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC design tasks]
To make tube MPC work, one needs to:
\[
    \text{compute } \mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \text{tighten the nominal constraints},
    \qquad
    \text{formulate the nominal MPC problem}.
\]
Then one proves robust constraint satisfaction and robust stability of the closed-loop system.
\end{theorembox}

\subsubsection{Recall: robust invariant set}

Before using the set \(\mathcal E\), it is useful to recall the notion of robust positive
invariance. Consider an autonomous disturbed system
\[
    x(k+1)=g(x(k),w(k)),
    \qquad
    w(k)\in\mathcal W,
\]
or a closed-loop disturbed system
\[
    x(k+1)=g(x(k),\kappa(x(k)),w(k)),
\]
where the feedback law \(\kappa\) is fixed. A set \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\) is robust positively
invariant if, whenever the current state belongs to the set, the next state belongs to the same
set for every admissible disturbance.

\begin{theorembox}[Robust positive invariant set]
A set \(\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}\) is a robust positive invariant set for
\[
    x(k+1)=g(x(k),w(k)),
    \qquad
    w(k)\in\mathcal W,
\]
if
\[
    x\in\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    g(x,w)\in\mathcal O_{\mathcal W}
    \qquad
    \forall w\in\mathcal W.
\]
\end{theorembox}

There is an important difference between the invariant sets used previously as terminal sets and
the invariant set used here. Previously, one was often interested in a large robust invariant set,
because the goal was to maximize the terminal region in which a local controller can keep the
state feasible. In tube MPC, instead, one wants a small robust invariant set for the error
dynamics, because \(\mathcal E\) measures the size of the tube. A smaller \(\mathcal E\) means
less constraint tightening and therefore a larger feasible region for the nominal MPC problem.

\subsubsection{Noisy system trajectory: constant tube}

Given a nominal trajectory \(z_i\), the real trajectory can be written as
\[
    x_i=z_i+e_i.
\]
The exact error \(e_i\) is not known in advance, because it depends on the disturbance
sequence. However, if the error set \(\mathcal E\) is robust positively invariant and
\(e_0\in\mathcal E\), then
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall i\geq 0.
\]
Therefore the real state satisfies
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal E
    =
    \{z_i+e\mid e\in\mathcal E\}.
\]
This is why the construction is called tube MPC: the nominal trajectory is surrounded by a
constant tube with cross-section \(\mathcal E\).

    \caption{Noisy system trajectory with a constant tube. Given a nominal trajectory \(z_i\),

\subsubsection{Constraint tightening}

The goal is to guarantee
\[
    x_i\in\mathcal X,
    \qquad
    u_i\in\mathcal U,
\]
for all disturbance sequences
\[
    \{w_0,\ldots,w_{i-1}\}\in\mathcal W^i.
\]
Tube MPC achieves this while optimizing only the nominal dynamics
\[
    z(k+1)=Az(k)+Bv(k).
\]
The noisy system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+Bu(k)+w(k)
\]
is kept feasible by tightening the constraints imposed on \(z_i\) and \(v_i\).\\

For the state constraint, since
\[
    x_i\in z_i\oplus\mathcal E,
\]
we need
\[
    z_i\oplus\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal X.
\]
By definition of the Pontryagin difference, this is equivalent to
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E.
\]
Thus the nominal state must satisfy the tightened state constraint
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E.
\]
The same argument applies to the input. Since
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i)=v_i+Ke_i
\]
and
\[
    e_i\in\mathcal E,
\]
the possible inputs satisfy
\[
    u_i\in v_i\oplus K\mathcal E.
\]
Therefore robust satisfaction of \(u_i\in\mathcal U\) is equivalent to
\[
    v_i\oplus K\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal U,
\]
or
\[
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Constraint tightening for tube MPC]
The necessary and sufficient tightening conditions are
\[
    z_i\oplus\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal X
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
\]
and
\[
    v_i\oplus K\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal U
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]
Since \(\mathcal E\) is computed offline, the tightened constraints
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
\]
can also be computed offline.
\end{theorembox}

\subsection{Tube MPC formulation and assumptions}

\subsubsection{Optimization problem}

We can now formulate the tube MPC optimization problem. The decision variables are the nominal
state sequence
\[
    Z=\{z_0,\ldots,z_N\}
\]
and the nominal input sequence
\[
    V=\{v_0,\ldots,v_{N-1}\}.
\]
For a measured state \(x_0\), the feasible set is defined as
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal Z(x_0)
    :=
    \Bigl\{(Z,V)\ \Big|\ 
    &
    z_{i+1}=Az_i+Bv_i,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E,
    \qquad i=0,\ldots,N-1,
    \\
    &
    z_N\in\mathcal X_f,
    \\
    &
    x_0\in z_0\oplus\mathcal E
    \Bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The cost function is evaluated on the nominal tube centers:
\[
    J(Z,V)
    :=
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(z_i,v_i)+\ell_f(z_N).
\]
The optimization problem is
\[
    (V^\star(x_0),Z^\star(x_0))
    =
    \argmin_{V,Z}
    \{J(Z,V)\mid (Z,V)\in\mathcal Z(x_0)\}.
\]
The applied control law is
\[
    \mu_{\mathrm{tube}}(x)
    :=
    K\bigl(x-z_0^\star(x)\bigr)+v_0^\star(x).
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC problem]
Tube MPC solves
\[
    (V^\star(x_0),Z^\star(x_0))
    =
    \argmin_{V,Z}
    \left\{
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(z_i,v_i)+\ell_f(z_N)
    \ \middle|\ 
    (Z,V)\in\mathcal Z(x_0)
    \right\},
\]
where \(\mathcal Z(x_0)\) is defined by the nominal dynamics, the tightened constraints
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E,
\]
the terminal condition \(z_N\in\mathcal X_f\), and the tube-inclusion condition
\[
    x_0\in z_0\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
The implemented input is
\[
    u=\mu_{\mathrm{tube}}(x)
    =
    K(x-z_0^\star(x))+v_0^\star(x).
\]
\end{theorembox}

This formulation differs from robust constraint-tightening MPC in an important way. The first
tube center \(z_0\) is an optimization variable; it is not forced to be equal to the measured state.
The only requirement is that the measured state lies inside the first tube:
\[
    x_0\in z_0\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
The cost is with respect to the nominal tube centers, while robustness is enforced through the
tightened constraints and the invariant error tube.

\subsubsection{Terminal assumptions}

The assumptions needed for tube MPC are essentially the nominal MPC assumptions applied to
the tightened nominal system. First, the stage cost is positive definite: it is strictly positive
away from the origin and equal to zero at the origin.\\

Second, the terminal set \(\mathcal X_f\) is invariant for the nominal system under a local
terminal controller
\[
    \kappa_f(z).
\]
That is,
\[
    Az+B\kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal X_f
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]
Moreover, the terminal set and terminal controller must satisfy the tightened constraints:
\[
    \mathcal X_f\subseteq\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
\]
\[
    \kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]
Third, the terminal cost is a continuous Lyapunov function in the terminal set:
\[
    \ell_f(Az+B\kappa_f(z))-\ell_f(z)
    \leq
    -\ell(z,\kappa_f(z)),
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC assumptions]
Tube MPC assumes:
\[
    \ell(z,v)>0
    \quad \text{for } (z,v)\neq(0,0),
\]
\[
    Az+B\kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal X_f
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f,
\]
\[
    \mathcal X_f\subseteq\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f,
\]
and
\[
    \ell_f(Az+B\kappa_f(z))-\ell_f(z)
    \leq
    -\ell(z,\kappa_f(z))
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]
\end{theorembox}

\subsection{Robust invariance and stability}

\subsubsection{Robust invariance}

We now prove the first main property: robust invariance of the feasible set. Define
\[
    \mathcal Z
    :=
    \{x\mid \mathcal Z(x)\neq\emptyset\}.
\]
This is the set of all states for which the tube MPC problem is feasible.

\begin{theorembox}[Robust invariance of tube MPC]
The set
\[
    \mathcal Z=\{x\mid \mathcal Z(x)\neq\emptyset\}
\]
is a robust positively invariant set for the closed-loop system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+B\mu_{\mathrm{tube}}(x(k))+w(k),
    \qquad
    w(k)\in\mathcal W,
\]
subject to the constraints
\[
    x(k)\in\mathcal X,
    \qquad
    u(k)\in\mathcal U.
\]
\end{theorembox}

Let
\[
    \bigl(\{v_0^\star,\ldots,v_{N-1}^\star\},
    \{z_0^\star,\ldots,z_N^\star\}\bigr)
\]
be the optimal solution for \(x(k)\). The applied input is
\[
    \mu_{\mathrm{tube}}(x(k))
    =
    K(x(k)-z_0^\star)+v_0^\star.
\]
The next state is therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
    x(k+1)
    &=
    Ax(k)+BK(x(k)-z_0^\star)+Bv_0^\star+w(k)       \\
    &=
    Az_0^\star+Bv_0^\star
    +
    A_K(x(k)-z_0^\star)+w(k)                       \\
    &=
    z_1^\star+A_Ke_0+w(k),
\end{aligned}
\]
where
\[
    e_0=x(k)-z_0^\star.
\]
Because the initial tube constraint gives
\[
    x(k)\in z_0^\star\oplus\mathcal E,
\]
we have
\[
    e_0\in\mathcal E.
\]
Since \(\mathcal E\) is robust positively invariant,
\[
    A_Ke_0+w(k)\in\mathcal E.
\]
Hence
\[
    x(k+1)\in z_1^\star\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
This means that \(z_1^\star\) is a valid first tube center for the next measured state.\\

As in nominal MPC, we can therefore construct a feasible candidate for the next optimization
problem by shifting the previous nominal sequence and appending the terminal controller:
\[
    \{v_1^\star,\ldots,v_{N-1}^\star,\kappa_f(z_N^\star)\},
\]
\[
    \{z_1^\star,\ldots,z_N^\star,Az_N^\star+B\kappa_f(z_N^\star)\}.
\]
The shifted sequence satisfies the nominal dynamics. The tightened state and input constraints
remain satisfied because they were already satisfied by the previous optimal solution, and the
terminal assumptions ensure that the appended terminal point and terminal input are feasible.
Therefore the tube MPC problem is feasible for every possible value of \(x(k+1)\) generated by
an admissible disturbance.

\subsubsection{Robust stability}

The second main property is robust stability. Since the disturbance is persistent, the real state
does not generally converge to the origin. Instead, tube MPC guarantees convergence to the
invariant error set \(\mathcal E\).

\begin{theorembox}[Robust stability of tube MPC]
For the closed-loop system
\[
    x(k+1)=Ax(k)+B\mu_{\mathrm{tube}}(x(k))+w(k),
    \qquad
    w(k)\in\mathcal W,
\]
the state \(x(k)\) converges in the limit to the set \(\mathcal E\):
\[
    \lim_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(x(k),\mathcal E)=0.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The proof follows the standard MPC Lyapunov argument, but applied to the nominal tube centers.
Let
\[
    J^\star(x(k))
    =
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(z_i^\star,v_i^\star)+\ell_f(z_N^\star)
\]
be the optimal value at time \(k\). Using the shifted feasible candidate at time \(k+1\), we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
    J^\star(x(k+1))
    &\leq
    \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\ell(z_i^\star,v_i^\star)
    +
    \ell(z_N^\star,\kappa_f(z_N^\star))
    +
    \ell_f(z_{N+1})
    \\
    &=
    J^\star(x(k))
    -
    \ell(z_0^\star,v_0^\star)
    -
    \ell_f(z_N^\star)
    +
    \ell_f(z_{N+1})
    +
    \ell(z_N^\star,\kappa_f(z_N^\star)).
\end{aligned}
\]
By the terminal Lyapunov condition,
\[
    \ell_f(z_{N+1})-\ell_f(z_N^\star)
    \leq
    -\ell(z_N^\star,\kappa_f(z_N^\star)).
\]
Therefore,
\[
    J^\star(x(k+1))
    \leq
    J^\star(x(k))
    -
    \ell(z_0^\star,v_0^\star).
\]
The optimal value decreases by at least the first nominal stage cost. Since the stage cost is
positive definite, this implies
\[
    \lim_{k\to\infty} z_0^\star(x(k))=0.
\]
The real state, however, satisfies
\[
    x(k)\in z_0^\star(x(k))\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
Thus, as the tube center converges to the origin, the real state converges to the tube cross-section
centered at the origin:
\[
    \lim_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(x(k),\mathcal E)=0.
\]
This is the correct robust stability statement in the presence of persistent disturbances.

\subsection{Implementation and example}

\subsubsection{Offline and online implementation}

The final tube MPC algorithm can be separated into an offline design phase and an online
implementation phase. Offline, one first chooses a stabilizing feedback gain \(K\) such that
\[
    A+BK
\]
is Schur stable. Then one computes a robust positively invariant set \(\mathcal E\), ideally the
minimal set
\[
    \mathcal E=\mathcal F_\infty
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}A_K^j\mathcal W
\]
for the error dynamics
\[
    e(k+1)=A_Ke(k)+w(k).
\]
Next, one computes the tightened constraints
\[
    \widetilde{\mathcal X}:=\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathcal U}:=\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]
Finally, one chooses the terminal cost \(\ell_f\) and terminal constraint \(\mathcal X_f\)
satisfying the tube MPC assumptions above.\\

Online, at each sampling instant, the current state \(x\) is measured or estimated. Then the
tube MPC problem is solved:
\[
    (V^\star(x),Z^\star(x))
    =
    \argmin_{V,Z}
    \{J(Z,V)\mid (Z,V)\in\mathcal Z(x)\}.
\]
The applied input is
\[
    u=K(x-z_0^\star(x))+v_0^\star(x).
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC algorithm]
Offline:
\[
    K
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \widetilde{\mathcal X}=\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,\quad
    \widetilde{\mathcal U}=\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \ell_f,\mathcal X_f.
\]
Online:
\[
    x
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    (V^\star(x),Z^\star(x))
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    u=K(x-z_0^\star(x))+v_0^\star(x).
\]
\end{theorembox}

\begin{remark}
It is often not possible to compute the minimal robust invariant set exactly, because it may have
infinitely many facets. In practice, one therefore uses a robust invariant outer approximation.
This approximation is slightly larger than the minimal set, which makes the tube larger and the
tightened constraints more conservative.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Example data}

We now illustrate the tube MPC construction on the double-integrator. The uncertain system is
\[
    x(k+1)
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 1\\
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}x(k)
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1\\
        0.5
    \end{bmatrix}u(k)
    +
    w(k),
\]
where the disturbance is bounded in the set
\[
    \mathcal W
    :=
    \{w\mid |w_1|\leq 0.01,\ |w_2|\leq 0.1\}.
\]
The state and input constraints are
\[
    \mathcal X
    :=
    \{x\mid \norm{x}_\infty\leq 3\},
    \qquad
    \mathcal U
    :=
    \{u\mid \norm{u}\leq 0.5\}.
\]
The stage cost is quadratic:
\[
    \ell(z,v)
    :=
    z^\top Qz+v^\top Rv,
\]
with
\[
    Q=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0\\
        0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    R=10.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC example data]
The example uses a disturbed double integrator with bounded additive disturbance
\[
    w\in\mathcal W,
\]
box state constraints
\[
    \norm{x}_\infty\leq 3,
\]
a bounded input constraint
\[
    \norm{u}\leq 0.5,
\]
and quadratic nominal stage cost
\[
    \ell(z,v)=z^\top Qz+v^\top Rv.
\]
\end{theorembox}

This example is useful because it shows all the main ingredients of tube MPC: the computation
of an invariant error set, the tightening of state and input constraints, the choice of terminal
ingredients, and finally the behavior of the planned and realized tubes.

\subsubsection{Computing the invariant error set}

The first offline step is to choose a stabilizing feedback gain \(K\) such that
\[
    A_K:=A+BK
\]
is Schur stable. In the example, \(K\) is chosen as the LQR gain for
\[
    Q=I,
    \qquad
    R=10.
\]
This gives
\[
    K=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -0.2311 & -0.7011
    \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The corresponding error dynamics are
\[
    e(k+1)=A_Ke(k)+w(k),
    \qquad
    w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
The next step is to compute a robust positively invariant set \(\mathcal E\) for this error
system. Ideally, one would choose the minimal RPI set
\[
    \mathcal E=\mathcal F_\infty
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}A_K^j\mathcal W.
\]
This set contains all possible steady-state errors generated by the persistent disturbance under
the stabilizing feedback \(K\).\\

The role of this set is fundamental. Once
\[
    e(k)\in\mathcal E,
\]
robust invariance guarantees
\[
    e(k+1)=A_Ke(k)+w(k)\in\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall w(k)\in\mathcal W.
\]
Thus the real state remains inside the tube
\[
    x(k)\in z(k)\oplus\mathcal E.
\]

\begin{remark}
The minimal robust invariant set may not be exactly computable with a finite number of
polyhedral facets. In implementations one often uses an invariant outer approximation. This
outer approximation is slightly larger than the minimal set, which makes the tube larger and the
tightened constraints more conservative.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Tightening the state constraints}

After computing the error set \(\mathcal E\), the next offline step is to tighten the state
constraints. In the example,
\[
    \mathcal X
    =
    \{x\mid \norm{x}_\infty\leq 3\}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    \mathcal X
    =
    \left\{
    x\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        I\\
        -I
    \end{bmatrix}
    x
    \leq
    3
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf 1\\
        \mathbf 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\}.
\]
If the error set is described as
\[
    \mathcal E=\{e\mid Fe\leq f\},
\]
then the tightened state constraint is
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E
    =
    \{x\mid x+e\in\mathcal X\ \forall e\in\mathcal E\}.
\]
Substituting the halfspace description of \(\mathcal X\), this becomes
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E
    =
    \left\{
    x\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        I\\
        -I
    \end{bmatrix}x
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        I\\
        -I
    \end{bmatrix}e
    \leq
    3
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf 1\\
        \mathbf 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \quad
    \forall e\in\mathcal E
    \right\}.
\]
Equivalently, each inequality is tightened by the worst possible error in the corresponding
normal direction:
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E
    =
    \left\{
    x\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        I\\
        -I
    \end{bmatrix}x
    \leq
    \begin{bmatrix}
        3-\max\{[1\ 0]e\mid e\in\mathcal E\}\\
        3-\max\{[0\ 1]e\mid e\in\mathcal E\}\\
        3-\max\{[-1\ 0]e\mid e\in\mathcal E\}\\
        3-\max\{[0\ -1]e\mid e\in\mathcal E\}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\}.
\]
The maximizations are linear programs and can be computed offline. The result is a polytope
with a smaller right-hand side than the original constraint set.

\begin{theorembox}[Polyhedral state constraint tightening]
If
\[
    \mathcal X=\{x\mid H_xx\leq h_x\},
    \qquad
    \mathcal E=\{e\mid Fe\leq f\},
\]
then
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E
    =
    \{x\mid H_xx\leq h_x-\eta_x\},
\]
where the \(j\)-th component of \(\eta_x\) is
\[
    (\eta_x)_j
    =
    \max_{e\in\mathcal E}H_{x,j}e.
\]
Each component is obtained by solving an offline linear program.
\end{theorembox}

    \centering
    % Replace the file name with the corresponding slide/image file.
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/offline_tightened_state_constraints.png}
    \caption{State constraint tightening. The original constraint set \(\mathcal X\) (blue) is shrunk by
    the error set \(\mathcal E\) (red). The tightened set \(\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E\) contains the
    admissible nominal tube centers. If \(z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E\) (green), then
    \(z_i\oplus\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal X\).}
    \label{fig:offline_tightened_state_constraints}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Tightening the input constraints}

The input constraint is tightened in the same way. The real input is
\[
    u_i=v_i+Ke_i,
    \qquad
    e_i\in\mathcal E.
\]
Therefore the set of possible real inputs around the nominal input \(v_i\) is
\[
    v_i\oplus K\mathcal E.
\]
To guarantee \(u_i\in\mathcal U\) for all admissible errors, we impose
\[
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]

For the scalar input constraint in the example,
\[
    \mathcal U
    =
    \left\{
    u\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1\\
        -1
    \end{bmatrix}u
    \leq
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.5\\
        0.5
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\},
\]
and if
\[
    \mathcal E=\{e\mid Fe\leq f\},
\]
then
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    &=
    \left\{
    u\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1\\
        -1
    \end{bmatrix}u
    \leq
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.5\\
        0.5
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\}
    \ominus
    \{Ke\mid Fe\leq f\}                                      \\
    &=
    \left\{
    u\ \middle|\ 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1\\
        -1
    \end{bmatrix}u
    \leq
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0.5-\max\{Ke\mid Fe\leq f\}\\
        0.5+\min\{Ke\mid Fe\leq f\}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Again, the required extrema are computed offline by linear programs.

\begin{theorembox}[Input constraint tightening]
The tightened input set is
\[
    \mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    =
    \{v\mid v+Ke\in\mathcal U\ \forall e\in\mathcal E\}.
\]
For polyhedral \(\mathcal U\) and \(\mathcal E\), this is obtained by tightening each input
inequality by the worst possible value of \(Ke\) in that inequality direction.
\end{theorembox}

\subsubsection{Terminal weights and terminal constraints}

The last offline step is to choose the terminal cost \(\ell_f\) and the terminal set
\(\mathcal X_f\). These terminal ingredients must satisfy the same kind of conditions as in
nominal MPC, but now with respect to the tightened constraints.\\

First, the terminal set must be invariant for the nominal system under a local controller
\(\kappa_f\):
\[
    Az+B\kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal X_f
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]
Second, the terminal set must lie inside the tightened state constraint and the terminal input
must lie inside the tightened input constraint:
\[
    \mathcal X_f\subseteq\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
\]
\[
    \kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]
Third, the terminal cost must be a Lyapunov function on \(\mathcal X_f\):
\[
    \ell_f(Az+B\kappa_f(z))-\ell_f(z)
    \leq
    -\ell(z,\kappa_f(z))
    \qquad
    \forall z\in\mathcal X_f.
\]

\begin{theorembox}[Terminal ingredients for tube MPC]
The terminal pair \((\ell_f,\mathcal X_f)\) must be chosen so that
\[
    Az+B\kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal X_f,
\]
\[
    \mathcal X_f\subseteq\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \kappa_f(z)\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E,
\]
and
\[
    \ell_f(Az+B\kappa_f(z))-\ell_f(z)
    \leq
    -\ell(z,\kappa_f(z)).
\]
These are the standard nominal MPC terminal conditions applied to the tightened nominal
system.
\end{theorembox}

A standard choice is to base the terminal ingredients on the LQR controller. One sets
\[
    \kappa_f(z)=Kz,
\]
where the same weights \(Q\) and \(R\) used in the MPC stage cost are used to compute \(K\).
The infinite-horizon cost of the LQR closed loop is
\[
    J^\star(z_0)
    =
    \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}
    z_i^\top(Q+K^\top RK)z_i
    =
    z_0^\top Pz_0,
\]
where \(P\) is the solution of the discrete-time Riccati equation. Therefore one can choose
\[
    \ell_f(z)=z^\top Pz.
\]
The set \(\mathcal X_f\) is then chosen as a positively invariant set for
\[
    z^+=(A+BK)z
\]
contained inside the tightened constraints. This is exactly the same construction used in
nominal MPC, but applied after state and input tightening.

\subsubsection{Online implementation}

Once all offline ingredients have been computed, the online implementation is simple. At each
sampling time, one measures or estimates the current state \(x\), solves the tube MPC problem
\[
    (V^\star(x),Z^\star(x))
    =
    \argmin_{V,Z}
    \{J(Z,V)\mid (Z,V)\in\mathcal Z(x)\},
\]
and applies
\[
    u=K(x-z_0^\star(x))+v_0^\star(x).
\]
Thus the online problem remains a nominal MPC problem in the variables \(Z,V\), but the
implemented input contains the feedback correction around the optimized tube center.

\begin{theorembox}[Offline/online tube MPC implementation]
Offline:
\[
    K
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \widetilde{\mathcal X}:=\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,\quad
    \widetilde{\mathcal U}:=\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \ell_f,\mathcal X_f.
\]
Online:
\[
    x
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    (V^\star(x),Z^\star(x))
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    u=K(x-z_0^\star(x))+v_0^\star(x).
\]
\end{theorembox}

\[

Around each center \(z_i\), the tube cross-section is the invariant error set \(\mathcal E\). Hence
the possible real states lie in
\[
    z_i\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
The planned tubes are contained in the original state constraints because the nominal centers

\subsection{Tube MPC summary}

Tube MPC can be summarized as a structured way to include feedback inside robust MPC while
keeping the online optimization problem simple. The input is split into two parts:
\[
    u_i=v_i+K e_i,
    \qquad
    e_i=x_i-z_i.
\]
The nominal input \(v_i\) is optimized online and steers the nominal system. The feedback part
\(Ke_i\) compensates for disturbances and keeps the real trajectory close to the nominal one.\\

The main benefit is that, after the offline computation of \(\mathcal E\), only one constraint
tightening is needed:
\[
    \widetilde{\mathcal X}=\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathcal U}=\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]
The online problem has essentially the same structure as nominal MPC, and the same terminal
cost and terminal set ideas can be used. Moreover, nominal MPC theory applies to the tube
centers, which gives a clean proof of convergence of the nominal trajectory.\\

The price is conservatism. Tube MPC is generally suboptimal compared with a fully optimal
closed-loop robust controller, because optimizing over arbitrary feedback policies is extremely
difficult. The feasible set is also smaller than in nominal MPC, since constraints must be
tightened. Finally, one must know a disturbance set \(\mathcal W\), and this is often unrealistic
or conservative in practice.

\begin{theorembox}[Tube MPC: main message]
Tube MPC splits the input into
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i).
\]
It optimizes the nominal trajectory and uses the feedback term to keep all disturbed trajectories
inside a tube. The method is simple online and has strong stability properties, but it is
conservative and requires a known disturbance set \(\mathcal W\).
\end{theorembox}

\section{Comparison of Robust Constraint-Tightening MPC and Tube MPC}

The two robust MPC approaches described in this chapter have the same objective: compensate
for disturbances in the prediction so that all constraints are satisfied by the real system. They
differ in how they represent the possible deviation between the nominal and real trajectories.\\

Robust constraint-tightening MPC uses disturbance reachable sets
\[
    \mathcal F_k
    =
    \bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}A_K^j\mathcal W.
\]
The nominal MPC problem uses time-varying tightened constraints
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_k,
\]
and the initial nominal state is fixed to the measured state:
\[
    z_0=x(k).
\]
This approach exploits the fact that the initial prediction error is zero. The tube grows along
the prediction horizon as more disturbances may accumulate.\\

Tube MPC instead uses a robust positively invariant set
\[
    \mathcal E.
\]
The nominal MPC problem uses constant tightened constraints
\[
    \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
\]
and the initial tube center is not necessarily equal to the measured state. Instead, it must
satisfy
\[
    x(k)\in z_0\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
This formulation is simple and has strong recursive feasibility and stability properties, but the
constant tube can be more conservative.

\begin{theorembox}[Robust MPC for uncertain systems]
Constraint-tightening MPC:
\[
    \text{DRS } \mathcal F_k
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{nominal MPC with } \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal F_k,
    \qquad
    z_0=x(k).
\]
Tube MPC:
\[
    \text{RPI set } \mathcal E
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \text{nominal MPC with } \mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    x(k)\in z_0\oplus\mathcal E.
\]
\end{theorembox}

The use of an RPI set \(\mathcal E\) increases the complexity of the offline design and may also
complicate the online implementation, depending on the representation of the set. Tube MPC can
also have a smaller feasible set, because the same tube cross-section is imposed throughout the
horizon. On the other hand, the concept is simple, the online problem remains close to nominal
MPC, and the robust stability properties are strong.

\section{Summary}

The main idea of robust MPC for uncertain systems is to compensate for noise in the prediction
so that the real state and input satisfy the constraints for all admissible disturbances. This
requires replacing single predicted trajectories with sets of possible trajectories, or tubes.\\

The robust methods discussed here are conservative because they require a disturbance set
\(\mathcal W\) that contains all possible disturbances. If \(\mathcal W\) is chosen too small, the
guarantees may fail. If it is chosen too large, the controller becomes overly cautious and the
feasible set may become small. Moreover, robust MPC formulations are generally more complex
than nominal MPC, even when the final online problem is simple.\\

The main benefit is that the feasible set becomes robust invariant. Therefore one knows exactly
from which states the controller can guarantee constraint satisfaction for all future times. The
methods are also easier to tune in a systematic way, because the designer can trade off robustness
against performance through the choice of \(\mathcal W\), the feedback gain \(K\), and the tube
size.\\

For tube MPC in particular, the central construction is
\[
    u_i=v_i+K(x_i-z_i),
\]
with an invariant error set
\[
    \mathcal E
\]
and tightened nominal constraints
\[
    z_i\in\mathcal X\ominus\mathcal E,
    \qquad
    v_i\in\mathcal U\ominus K\mathcal E.
\]
Under the terminal assumptions, the nominal tube center converges to the origin, while the real
state converges to the robust invariant set \(\mathcal E\). Thus robust MPC replaces exact
convergence to the origin by convergence to a guaranteed neighborhood of the origin, which is
the natural stability objective in the presence of persistent disturbances.
